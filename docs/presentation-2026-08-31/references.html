References — journal papers only

Every entry below is a peer-reviewed journal article from an IEEE Transactions title or a Web of Science / Scopus indexed journal. No conference papers, no preprint-only work, no blog posts.

Each one has a freely downloadable author version saved in this folder, so the PDFs travel with the presentation and the panel can read any of them immediately. The author version is the authors' own copy of the same paper — identical content, different typesetting from the publisher's copy — and is what the publishers allow authors to share.

Why each one is in the deck

  • R1 LogFiT — a language model fine-tuned on logs, published in an IEEE Transactions. It is the honest replacement for the old "LLM-based log analysis (recent work)" row, which cited no actual paper. It is also the closest published work to the approach the original proposal described, and it reports its results on clean data only.
  • R2 Landauer et al. survey — the field-wide survey of deep learning for log anomaly detection. Used on the Background slide: it shows what the field measures, and that robustness against a deliberately hiding attacker is not part of it.
  • R3 Ali et al. — compares classical machine learning against deep learning on log data and finds simpler models competitive. This is the published support for choosing Isolation Forest over a large trained model, instead of it looking like a shortcut.
  • R4 Wu, Li & Khomh — measures how much the choice of log representation (including sentence embeddings) matters. Directly supports the embedding step in our pipeline.
  • R5 Khan et al. — shows how much log parsing quality changes anomaly detection results. Supports why we spent effort on the parser, and why our parse rate (75.98% → 99.37% on Linux.log) is worth reporting.
  • R6 Pang et al. — the standard review of deep anomaly detection; supplies the definitions and the framing used on the Method slide.
  • R7 Kheddar — 2025 survey of transformers and LLMs for intrusion detection. Shows the field is moving where we are, and that evaluation is still on static datasets.
  • R8 Debicha et al. — attackers deliberately evading a detector, measured. The single closest published work to our adversarial track, and it is on network traffic rather than logs, which is exactly the gap we point at.

The shared limitation these papers reveal

All eight evaluate on data where the attacker is not trying to hide from the specific detector being tested. R8 is the only one with an active adversary, and it targets network intrusion detection rather than log analysis. None of them compares against a production SIEM ruleset under camouflage. That absence is our research gap, and it is citable rather than asserted.

Verification notes

  • Every DOI above was resolved through Crossref, and the title, authors, journal, volume, issue, pages and year printed here come from that record rather than from memory.
  • Every PDF was downloaded from the authors' own deposit: arXiv for R2–R8, and the CQUniversity institutional repository for R1. No paywalled publisher PDF was used.
  • Conference papers were deliberately excluded, which is why DeepLog (ACM CCS 2017) and LogBERT (IJCNN 2021) no longer appear in the literature table. If the panel asks about them, they are named in the knowledge pack as foundational conference work.
